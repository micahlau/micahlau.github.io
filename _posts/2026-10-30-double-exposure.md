I put an old roll of Fujifilm Superia, dug up from a dresser in my childhood room, through a camera over the past month, taking photos around the apartment every morning just to hear the shutter snap and to feel the tension of the advance lever, expecting no good results. Knowing the film was surely heat-damaged and would give faded, grainy images, I took pictures of the daily scenes of home – dishes in the sink, view out the window, my work desk or coffee cup, bedroom full of afternoon sun – a daily photojournal.  

Turns out the film had been shot once before. Every photograph was now a double exposure, light from the present superimposed upon light from the past, memory into memory. 

On a family trip to Japan in 2009, I took my grandfather's Canon AE-1 and could not figure out what the dials meant, gave up quickly in favor of my mother's PowerShot. A few film photos came out well after all; here is Nikkō Tōshōgū Shrine overlaid with my kitchen faucet and the view of the street as I look down from the window:

![](https://images.micahlau.com/2026-10-30/2.webp)

One image shook me. My father, his hair still black and his face full, looks into the lens as we wait in a train station. My mother of two decades ago has been gauzed, smiling, by the bright Honolulu skyline of today. Behind them, someone with a large ring on their index finger manipulates a flip phone, waiting for the train. 

![](https://images.micahlau.com/2026-10-30/3.webp)

My parents are being photographed by their fifteen-year-old son. What do they see?
